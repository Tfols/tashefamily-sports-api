§ 01

How to Read This Catalogue

This guide catalogues 34 distinct American regional and local pizza styles, organized into five geographic clusters (Northeast, Midwest, Appalachia/Mid-Atlantic, West, and a smaller Gulf Coast/islands group), plus a dedicated treatment of the household's specific Erie question and a section on searches that came up empty. It is the companion to a shorter Field Checklist built for scanning; this document carries the full history, credited originators, and sourcing behind every entry.

Five styles in this catalogue are already familiar to this household: New York style, Neapolitan, Chicago deep dish, Detroit style (a particular favorite, made at home), and New Haven/Connecticut apizza. The other 29 are organized to surface exactly what was asked for — the small-town and obscure regional variants in the spirit of Erie and Old Forge, which make up the large majority of this catalogue.

Every entry carries a sourcing-confidence read, expressed consistently across this document:

Well-documentedMultiple independent sources agree on the core facts — named originator, place, and date, or a clearly corroborated style description.
Moderate / hedgedThe style itself is real and covered, but some specific detail (an exact date, an originator's identity) is disputed or softly sourced even among the sources that cover it.
Thin / unconfirmedReal signal that something exists, but thin, single-source, or explicitly hedged in the original coverage — presented as a lead, not a settled fact.
Confirmed negativeActively searched; multiple sources agree no distinct codified style exists. Different from "not yet researched" — this is a real finding, covered in § 17.

Several individual entries carry their own specific caveats — a disputed founding date, a contested "who invented it" claim, a charming-but-unverified shop legend. Those stay attached to their specific entry throughout this document rather than being smoothed into a single generic disclaimer at the end.

§ 02

The Erie Question — What We Actually Found

This household specifically asked about Erie-style pizza, alongside Old Forge, as an example of the small-town regional variant it wanted this catalogue to surface. The honest finding deserves to be stated plainly and up front rather than buried: no good, independent sourcing exists establishing "Erie style" as its own codified pizza tradition, distinct the way Old Forge, Quad Cities, or Brier Hill are documented.

What we went looking for
"Erie-Style" Pizza

No dedicated historical writeup treats "Erie style" as its own named tradition. What Erie actually shares in is the broader Western New York / Rust Belt cup-and-char pepperoni tradition centered on Buffalo — small-diameter pepperoni cups that curl and char at the edges, documented in depth back to WWII-era Buffalo pizzerias like Bocce Club (1946) and tied to Buffalo meat producer Battistoni Italian Specialty Meats (est. 1931). Erie shares in this old-school cup-and-char pepperoni tradition — it never fully switched to "lay-flat" pepperoni, similar to Cleveland and Detroit — and local shops like Lucky Man's Pizzeria are noted for it. But that's participation in a regional tradition centered elsewhere, not a separately codified "Erie style."

What Erie IS uniquely documented for
Pepperoni Balls

A fried, dough-wrapped pepperoni snack — a different food entirely, not a pizza style. Originated in Erie's Little Italy, reportedly evolving from Depression-era fried fish-scrap balls. The Mohican Market and Bakery was advertising them for sale as early as 1947. This is real, specific, and worth seeking out on its own terms — just not the thing the "Erie-style pizza" framing implies.

This is flagged as a genuine research gap, not a confident negative the way Seattle or Rochester are (see § 17). It's entirely possible a strong local source exists documenting "Erie style" as its own thing that simply didn't surface in this search. Worth a second, more targeted look if the household wants to pursue it further — but as it stands, the honest answer is: Erie's pizza tradition is Buffalo/WNY cup-and-char, and Erie's own genuine culinary original is a snack, not a pie.

§ 03

Vault Candidates — Three Pilgrimages Worth Planning

Three finds in this catalogue rise above "try the style" into "visit the actual place where it was invented." All three are still-operating, verifiable, named establishments — not just a style credited to a city.

Vault Candidate 1 · Trenton / Robbinsville, NJ

Papa's Tomato Pies (est. 1912)

Giuseppe "Joe" Papa, a 17-year-old Neapolitan immigrant, opened Papa's Tomato Pies in 1912 in Trenton's Chambersburg neighborhood — cited as the oldest continuously operating family-owned pizza restaurant in the country. The related De Lorenzo's lineage opened its own shop in 1947. Both defining trait: cheese and toppings go down first, with tomato sauce ladled on top afterward, the reverse of the usual order. Both Papa's and De Lorenzo's have since relocated from Trenton proper to nearby Robbinsville, NJ — worth noting before planning a visit to "Trenton" itself. See § 04 for the full Trenton tomato pie entry.

Vault Candidate 2 · Idaho Springs, CO

Beau Jo's (est. 1973)

Chip Bair invented the Colorado "mountain pie" — a thick, deliberately hand-braided crust that holds more toppings and turns pizza into a meal for four, sold by the pound, served with honey for crust-dipping — after buying Beau Jo's in Idaho Springs in 1973. The chain has since grown to six Colorado locations (Idaho Springs, Arvada, Evergreen, Longmont, Fort Collins, Steamboat Springs), but Idaho Springs is the original. This account comes straight from the restaurant's own history — generally reliable for founding facts, though not independently third-party verified. See § 15.

Vault Candidate 3 · Wooster Street, New Haven, CT

Frank Pepe Pizzeria Napoletana (1925) & Sally's Apizza (1938)

New Haven apizza is already a household favorite in style — this is the upgrade. Frank Pepe is credited with originating New Haven-style apizza in 1925; his nephew-by-training Salvatore Consiglio opened Sally's Apizza down the same street in 1938, still coal-fired more than 80 years later. The two originating establishments sit only a few blocks apart on Wooster Street. Frank Pepe himself is specifically credited with the white clam pie in the 1960s. See § 05.

§ 04

New York / Long Island / New Jersey Cluster

New York style — New York City · Already Tried

Thin, hand-tossed, foldable crust with a light layer of low-moisture mozzarella, sold by the slice from large pies, traditionally baked in coal or gas deck ovens. Traces to Gennaro Lombardi, who began selling tomato pies wrapped in paper at his Little Italy grocery around 1897 and received the first pizzeria license in the U.S. in 1905, using a coal-fired oven. Coal (burning up to 1000°F, versus wood's 600–800°F) produced the bigger, chewier, more heavily charred pies with more toppings — including pepperoni, an American invention — that became the NY template. Try: Lombardi's, Totonno's, John's of Bleecker Street, Grimaldi's — all NYC, all historically cited originators.

Neapolitan / Neapolitan-American — Already Tried

True Neapolitan (VPN-certified, per the Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana) requires a wood-fired dome oven around 900°F and a four-ingredient dough (00 flour, water, salt, fresh yeast); coal, electric, and gas-combo ovens are explicitly disallowed by the certifying body. Early American immigrant pizzerias could never have met this standard, since they used American flour, dairy, and coal-fired ovens instead of wood — precisely how "American Neapolitan" (i.e. New York style) diverged from true Neapolitan in the first place.

The modern American "neo-Neapolitan"/"NEOpolitan" wave — wood-fired, blistered, VPN-adjacent pizzerias now common in many U.S. cities — is a distinct, more recent phenomenon self-consciously imitating Naples, not a lineage descendant of the early-1900s immigrant pizzerias.

Grandma pizza — Long Island

Thin, square, crisp-bottomed pizza baked in an oiled sheet pan — historically a home-kitchen style, hence the name. Credited to Umberto Corteo, who opened Umberto's in New Hyde Park in 1965; the name reportedly came from a regular customer, Anthony "Tippy" Nocella, in the 1980s, and the "Sicilian Grandma" variant was introduced around 1994. Well documented, though the naming legend is plausible rather than airtight. Try: Umberto's, multiple Long Island locations, credited as originator.

Sicilian pizza (American) vs. sfincione

American "Sicilian" pizza — thick, square, focaccia-like, heavy on mozzarella — descends from sfincione but diverged sharply. Authentic sfincione is topped with breadcrumbs, anchovies, tuma or caciocavallo cheese, onions, and a meatless tomato sauce, often applied in reverse order. The American version emerged because caciocavallo was hard to source in the U.S. while cow's-milk mozzarella was cheap and reliable; culinary historians place the adaptation in Brooklyn, likely during the Depression. Well documented as a lineage/divergence story, though the exact origin point is hedged ("culinary researchers believe") rather than pinned to a single named shop.

Trenton tomato pie — Trenton / Robbinsville, NJ · Vault Candidate

Circular, thin-crust pie distinguished by order of assembly: cheese and toppings go down first, with tomato sauce ladled on top afterward. Traces to Giuseppe "Joe" Papa's Papa's Tomato Pies (1912) — see § 03 — with the related De Lorenzo's lineage opening its own shop in 1947. Well documented, with a specific, verifiable founding date. Both establishments have since relocated from Trenton proper to nearby Robbinsville, NJ.

Jersey Shore / boardwalk pizza — Jersey Shore, NJ

A second, genuinely distinct New Jersey style, not to be confused with Trenton tomato pie despite the shared home state. Order of assembly runs the other direction: mozzarella cheese goes down on the dough first, followed by a spiral or swirl of tangy tomato sauce ladled on top — traditionally from copper kettles — producing a thin, crisp crust built to hold up in humid, seasonal boardwalk conditions. Traces to the post-WWII boardwalk food boom along the Jersey Shore, and spans multiple towns and establishments rather than one credited originating shop. Well documented across multiple independent food/travel outlets and a dedicated regional-history book (Karen L. Schnitzspahn's Jersey Shore Food History: Victorian Feasts to Boardwalk Treats) — but unlike New Haven apizza or Old Forge tray pizza, no dedicated pizza-historian deep-dive treats it as its own formally codified style, so it's presented here as well-documented but less formally codified than this catalogue's most rigorously-sourced entries. Try: Manco & Manco (Ocean City/Somers Point, since 1956) and Maruca's (Seaside Heights/Seaside Park) — commonly cited examples spanning the tradition rather than a single point of origin.

Buffalo style — Buffalo, NY

Medium-thick, pan-baked dough; sweet, tomato-paste-forward sauce edge to edge; heavy whole-milk mozzarella; signature cup-and-char pepperoni piled to the rim. No single credited inventor — the style evolved across multiple postwar pizzerias — though Santora's Pizzeria (Joseph Santora) is sometimes cited for popularizing the thick-pan approach using a repurposed GM factory steel dish. Well documented as a style; looser sourcing on "who invented it first." Try: Bocce Club Pizza (1946, flagship of the tradition), La Nova, Santora's (Depew, opened 1970).

§ 05

New England

New Haven / Connecticut apizza — New Haven, Wooster Street · Already Tried · Vault Candidate

Thin, irregularly-shaped, coal-fired crust, blistered and charred, often sparse or no mozzarella unless requested, famously topped with clams (the "white clam pie"). Frank Pepe is credited with originating New Haven-style apizza in 1925; his nephew-by-training Salvatore Consiglio opened Sally's Apizza down the same street in 1938, still coal-fired 80+ years later. The white clam pie specifically is credited to Frank Pepe himself in the 1960s. Well documented, with named founders and dates. See § 03 for the pilgrimage case.

Greek pizza / "New England style" — New London, CT

Proofed and baked in an oiled metal pan rather than stretched to order, giving an oily, fried, crisp-bottomed crust; typically topped with a 50/50 mozzarella–white cheddar blend. Despite the name, not Greek in culinary origin — credited to Costas "Charlie" Kitsatis, a Greek immigrant, at Pizza House in New London in 1955, part of a broader wave in which Greek immigrant families took over pizzerias from retiring Italian owners across New England. Well documented, with a named originator, restaurant, and date.

South Shore bar pizza — Massachusetts, south of Boston

A Sicilian-Greek hybrid: small (~10-inch), thin, cracker-like crust baked in individual pans, mozzarella-cheddar blend stretched to the pan's edge for a charred, lacy border; strongly associated with Portuguese-heritage toppings like linguiça. The Lynwood in Randolph is cited as an early (circa 1949) pioneer, run by a family of Lithuanian immigrant descent. Well documented, and currently having a cultural moment — Boston Globe and Boston Magazine features from 2020–2024. Try: The Lynwood (Randolph, MA), cited as the pioneering location.

Massachusetts "beach pizza" — Lawrence / Salisbury Beach, MA

Distinct from South Shore bar pizza: square, thin-crust pizza with sweet tomato sauce and a very light dusting of cheese. Credited to Angelo Zappala at Tripoli Bakery in Lawrence in 1944, transplanted to Salisbury Beach in summer 1945; rival Cristy's opened at Salisbury Beach in March 1946, and the two have coexisted in a friendly rivalry ever since. Well documented, with named founders and specific dates. Try: Tripoli Bakery and Cristy's, both at Salisbury Beach — essentially the only two places that make the authentic style.

Rhode Island pizza strips — Providence, Federal Hill

Cheese-less (or minimal-cheese), thickly-sauced focaccia-style bread baked in sheet pans, cooled, cut into rectangular strips, and eaten at room temperature — a party/graduation food. Believed to have evolved from home-baked focaccia traditions Italian immigrants brought to Federal Hill beginning in the early 1900s. Well documented. Try: Palmieri's Bakery (Providence, since 1898/incorporated 1901) and LaSalle Bakery (a Providence institution since 1932).

§ 06

Chicago's Four Pizza Traditions

Deep dish · Already Tried

Thick, high-walled crust in a round steel pan, layered cheese-then-toppings-then-chunky-sauce-on-top, eaten with a fork and knife. Credited to Pizzeria Uno (opened 1943 by Ike Sewell and Ric Riccardo), though who actually invented the recipe is genuinely disputed among historians — Rudy Malnati Sr. (a Pizzeria Uno kitchen employee who later founded Lou Malnati's in 1971) claimed credit, as did Riccardo himself. The restaurant-of-origin is solid; the "who invented it" question remains openly unresolved even among food historians. Try: Pizzeria Uno/Due, Lou Malnati's, Gino's East — all well-established, though "true original" is contested among locals.

Tavern-style / thin crust, party-cut

Thin, unleavened, cracker-crisp dough cut into small squares ("tavern cut"/"party cut") rather than wedges, toppings to the edge. Emerged in Chicago taverns, likely in the 1930s–40s post-Prohibition, as a cheap bar snack meant to encourage drink sales — no single credited inventor. Well documented as a genre, thinner on any specific founding claim. Try: Vito & Nick's and Pat's Pizza are commonly cited — more "commonly cited" than independently verified, so treat as a lead rather than a confirmed originator.

Stuffed pizza

Distinct from deep dish, and frequently confused with it. Adds a second, thin top crust under the sauce, on top of even more cheese than deep dish; inspired by the Italian Easter pie scarciedda/pizza rustica. Both Nancy's Pizzeria and Giordano's (founded 1974) claim originator status, and the two have disputed the claim in court. Well documented as its own category, with a genuinely contested origin.

Pizza pot pie

The most obscure of the four. Sauce, sausage, mushrooms, and Wisconsin brick cheese layered in a ceramic bowl, topped with Sicilian dough, baked, and served flipped upside-down onto a plate. Documented as one of Chicago's five recognized pizza sub-styles (alongside deep dish, stuffed, pan, and tavern) but rarely found outside the city. Thinner sourcing than the other three — mentioned consistently as a category, but with less independent historical depth behind it.

§ 07

Detroit Style — The Red-Top/Blue-Top Question

Rectangular pan pizza, thick but airy crust, crisp caramelized cheese edges (cheese pushed to the pan's rim), historically made with Wisconsin brick cheese. Origin is well documented: Buddy's Rendezvous Pizzeria, Detroit's east side, 1946, credited to Gus and Anna Guerra with help from employee Connie Piccinato, using repurposed blue-steel automotive parts trays. Already tried by this household — a particular favorite, made at home.

"Red top" is a real, named variant — sauce ladled on top in stripes after baking, rather than under the cheese. No documentation was found of "blue top" as a competing sauce-placement style. "Blue top" refers to the blue steel pans themselves — a manufacturing detail, not a sauce style. The red-top/blue-top framing as a paired binary appears to conflate pan color/type with sauce placement; flagged here explicitly so it doesn't get repeated as fact.

Try: Buddy's Pizza (originator, multiple metro Detroit locations), Loui's Pizza, Cloverleaf, Shield's — early adopters and competitors in the same Detroit tradition.

§ 08

The Ohio Cluster

Four distinct, separately-documented styles within Ohio — not one "Ohio style."

Brier Hill-style — Brier Hill neighborhood, Youngstown

Breadlike, thick dough; thick tomato sauce; green bell peppers; Romano cheese instead of mozzarella. Developed by Italian immigrants during the Depression as a use for surplus bread dough, originally baked in communal brick ovens; Romano was used because it didn't require refrigeration. Well documented, including coverage in Atlas Obscura's Gastro Obscura. Try: Avalon Downtown Pizzeria (Youngstown), Wedgewood Pizza (Austintown/Boardman, Food Network recognized), Sunrise Pizza (Warren, OH).

Ohio Valley-style — Steubenville, OH, spreading into Wheeling, WV

Square crust with tomato sauce baked in, then cold, uncooked toppings and grated mozzarella added after baking. Credited specifically to Primo DiCarlo, a WWII veteran who tasted pizza in Italy and tried to recreate it back home, opening the Original DiCarlo's Famous Pizza Shop in 1945 — the first licensed pizza restaurant in Ohio. By the late 1950s there were reportedly 24 DiCarlo's shops across the Ohio Valley. Well documented, with a named founder, date, and verifiable expansion. Try: DiCarlo's (Steubenville original, plus Wheeling, WV) — the actual originating chain, still operating.

Cleveland-style

Thin crust cut into small squares, with a notably sweet, sugared tomato sauce as its signature trait. Emerged from early-20th-century Italian immigrant communities. Mama Roberto's (Mentor, OH) holds the legal trademark on "Cleveland Style Pizza" since 1999; Geraci's (1956) was singled out by chef Michael Symon in a widely-cited 2011 social media post as his favorite. Moderately documented — the trademark and Symon endorsement are solid; the deeper origin story is more general and inferential.

Columbus-style

Thin, cracker-like crust with a cornmeal-dusted base; sweet tomato sauce; edge-to-edge toppings; provolone cheese instead of mozzarella; square tavern cut. Credited to Jimmy Massey and Romeo Sirij in the early 1950s; the provolone choice is attributed to Columbus having had only one Italian ingredient supplier in the early pizza era, and that supplier stocked provolone rather than mozzarella. Well documented, with a dedicated Wikipedia entry and coverage across multiple food-history pieces. Try: Massey's Pizza and Rubino's — historic Columbus pizzerias.

Cincinnati and Louisville are sometimes described as sharing a loosely-defined "Ohio Valley" cold-topping tradition with Steubenville, but neither has its own confirmed distinct style. Louisville pizza-makers themselves, quoted directly in a Louisville alt-weekly, say there is no distinct Louisville style despite periodic attempts to coin one. Kansas City likewise has no broadly recognized signature style. See § 17 for the full confirmed-negative treatment of all three.

§ 09

St. Louis

St. Louis-style

Very thin, cracker-like, unleavened crust (no yeast); cut into squares ("party cut"); topped with Provel, a processed blend of cheddar, Swiss, and provolone (plus liquid smoke per some sourcing) that doesn't stretch like mozzarella. Provel was developed around 1947 by Hoffman Dairy (Wisconsin) specifically for St. Louis's Costa Grocery, aiming for a cheese with sharper flavor that wouldn't string. Imo's, opened by Ed Imo in 1964, is the dominant purveyor and effectively synonymous with the style nationally. Well documented on Provel's origin and Imo's founding date.

The square-cut choice is attributed, per shop legend, to Imo's prior work as a linoleum tile cutter. This reads as charming shop legend rather than rigorously sourced fact — flagged as such rather than repeated as settled history.

§ 10

Milwaukee

Milwaukee tavern-style

Cracker-thin (~1.25mm) crust cut into tavern squares, sauce and toppings to the edge. Traced to the Caradaro Club, opened in the Third Ward in 1945 by John Caravella and Joe Todaro, who deliberately combined Sicilian pizza's rectangular-slice tradition with Neapolitan thinness. Many later Milwaukee pizzerias (Balistreri's, Barbierie's, DiMarini's, Calderone Club, Lisa's) were founded by former Caradaro Club employees. Well documented, with a specific founding restaurant, date, and named founders. Conceptually similar to Chicago tavern-style, but an independently documented lineage — not a direct offshoot of it.

§ 11

Quad Cities (Iowa / Illinois Border)

Quad City-style

Dark, malt-flavored crust (malt syrup gives color and a nutty flavor); tomato sauce spiked with red chili flakes or cayenne; toppings placed under the cheese; cut into elongated strips rather than wedges or squares. Credited to Tony Maniscalco Sr., who arrived in the Quad Cities in 1952 and developed the style using a base recipe from the Bacino family, popularized mid-1950s. Well documented, with a dedicated Wikipedia entry. Try: Harris Pizza (since 1960, named a top Iowa pizza place by one national outlet), plus Uncle Bill's, Fields of Pizza (Moline), and Happy Joe's (Bettendorf, 1972, also credited as originator of taco pizza).

§ 12

Pennsylvania Coal-Region and Small-Town Styles

Old Forge-style — Old Forge, PA, "the Pizza Capital of the World"

Baked in rectangular metal pans and sold by the "tray" (whole or half) rather than the "pie," with pieces called "cuts" rather than slices. Light, crisp-bottomed, chewy-centered crust; comes in "red" (sweet sauce, mozzarella/American/cheddar blend) and "white" (double-crust, no sauce, cheese and fillings sandwiched between two dough layers) varieties. Reportedly dates to 1926, though this is a commonly repeated date rather than one anchored to a specific primary document. Well documented as a living, ongoing local tradition, with active "top 10 Old Forge pizzerias" industry press coverage, though less rigorously documented on precise founding year. Try: multiple Old Forge pizzerias are commonly cited — Arcaro & Genell, Revello's, and Salerno's among the most frequently named — but treat any single "best" ranking as opinion.

Altoona-style — Altoona, PA

Sicilian-style thick crust; sweet tomato sauce; a ring of green bell pepper; deli-style salami; and, most distinctively, a slice of American cheese or Velveeta on top. Originated as a menu item at the Altoona Hotel, likely in the 1950s (some sources say 1960s–70s — the exact decade is genuinely disputed across sources). The hotel burned down in 2013; the style now survives at other local spots that adopted it, including 29th Street Pizza and Zach's Sports Bar. Moderately well documented — multiple independent food-media pieces, including a Wikipedia entry — but the founding decade is explicitly unsettled even among those sources, flagged here as a genuine disagreement rather than resolved.

§ 13

Mid-Atlantic

Baltimore / Maryland-style

Thin, crispy crust; sweet tomato sauce with a noticeable sugar presence; provolone (not mozzarella) as the primary cheese; square/rectangular cut; sometimes topped with Maryland blue crab. Credited to Bob Beall, described as a tobacco farmer and former minor-league baseball player with no prior pizza experience, whose choice of rectangular pan was reportedly a practical accommodation (round pans were hard to find) rather than a stylistic choice.

Thinner sourcing than most entries in this catalogue — attributed to Beall across a couple of pieces but without a strong primary-source anchor (no clearly dated founding, no restaurant name tied to the claim). Treat "Bob Beall invented it" as a lead rather than an established fact.

§ 14

Upstate New York

Utica / Rome tomato pie — Utica, NY

Rectangular, thick, airy crust; hearty tomato sauce; pecorino Romano or Parmesan dusted on top; little to no mozzarella; served at room temperature. O'Scugnizzo's Pizzeria is credited with selling 5-cent tomato pies from a basement starting around 1914. Utica tomato pie sits alongside other Utica-originated Italian-American dishes like Utica greens and chicken riggies as part of a broader, well-documented regional food identity. Well documented. Try: Roma Sausage & Deli, described as the place that "put Utica tomato pie on the map."

Rochester, NY was searched specifically for a distinct Rochester bar-pizza or local style; none was found. Coverage there centers on standard New York-style pizza and Brooklyn-descended shops rather than any locally codified style. Confirmed negative finding — see § 17.

§ 15

The West

California-style

Thin crust with nontraditional, often high-end or produce-forward toppings (goat cheese, artichoke hearts, chicken with peanut sauce, etc.), breaking sharply from red-sauce-and-mozzarella convention. Credited to a specific circle of early-1980s California chefs: Ed LaDou (working first at Prego in San Francisco, then poached by Wolfgang Puck for Spago in Los Angeles in 1982) and Alice Waters at Chez Panisse in Berkeley. LaDou later worked with California Pizza Kitchen. Well documented, with named individuals, restaurants, and a specific year (1982).

Colorado-style "mountain pie" — Idaho Springs, CO · Vault Candidate

Thick, hand-braided crust (braiding is a defining visual trait); heavy sauce and cheese; sold by the pound; served with honey for crust-dipping. Invented by Chip Bair after he bought Beau Jo's in Idaho Springs in 1973 — see § 03 — deliberately hand-rolling crusts to hold more toppings and turn pizza into a meal for four. Well documented, with a single named originator, restaurant, and date, straight from the restaurant's own account (primary/self-reported source, generally reliable for founding facts but not independently third-party verified).

New Mexico green chile pizza

A topping/ingredient tradition — roasted green chile, sometimes blue corn crust, carne adovada — layered onto pizza rather than a single codified crust/sauce/cut style. Sourcing describes it as resisting "tidy definition," with "various interpretations across the state." A real regional culinary tendency, but not (yet) a documented, named pizza style the way Old Forge or Quad Cities are. Thin sourcing — flagged rather than presented as settled.

Seattle was actively searched and found to have no distinct codified style. Multiple local food-media pieces explicitly state Seattle "doesn't have its own regional style of pie a la New York or Chicago" and instead hosts a wide mix of imported styles (Neapolitan, Detroit, NY-inspired, Roman). Confirmed negative finding — see § 17.

§ 16

Gulf Coast, Islands & Other Thinly-Documented Traditions

New Orleans / Gulf Coast

Scattered mentions of Cajun-Italian fusion toppings at individual restaurants (e.g. a "9th Ward" pizza with Creole mustard aioli and gulf shrimp toppings) and a charming but very thinly sourced regional habit of dipping pizza slices in Catalina/French dressing on the Mississippi Gulf Coast. None of this rises to a codified, named regional pizza style — it's individual-restaurant creativity plus one single-source local eating habit. Treat the Catalina-dressing detail as a lead (one mention, not corroborated elsewhere), not a confirmed regional fact.

Puerto Rican-style pizza

Thin sourcing. General claims of "a distinct flavor with a thin crust that is not overly crispy or flaky," and San Juan's pizza culture growing since the 1970s, but no named founder, restaurant, or specific defining technique comparable to the mainland styles above. One genuinely well-defined distinct dish sits alongside this: piñón — a plantain-and-ground-beef layered pie, historically pan-cooked on the stovetop, more a savory plantain casserole than pizza in the crust-and-sauce sense — interesting, but arguably a different dish wearing "pizza" as a loose descriptor. Presented here as unconfirmed/thin.

Hawaiian pizza — a correction, not a regional entry

Hawaiian pizza did not originate in Hawaii and is not a documented Hawaiian regional tradition. It was invented in Chatham, Ontario, Canada, in 1962 by Sam Panopoulos, a Greek-Canadian restaurateur, and named after the brand of canned pineapple he used. This is well documented — multiple obituaries and profiles of Panopoulos corroborate it — but it firmly does not belong in a catalogue of American regional pizza traditions, since it's Canadian in origin. No evidence was found of a genuinely distinct Hawaii-based pizza tradition beyond the topping combination being served everywhere, including in Hawaii itself. Included here specifically to correct the record rather than to recommend a trip.

§ 17

Confirmed Negative Findings

Seven places were actively searched for a distinct, codified regional pizza style and came up empty. This is genuinely useful information — it can save a future search from chasing something that doesn't exist — and is presented here as its own section rather than folded quietly into the sourcing notes.

Searched, No Distinct Style Found
Seattle, WA

Multiple local food-media pieces explicitly state Seattle has no regional style of its own, hosting instead a wide mix of imported styles (Neapolitan, Detroit, NY-inspired, Roman).

Rochester, NY

Coverage centers on standard New York-style pizza and Brooklyn-descended shops rather than any locally codified style.

Kansas City, MO

Louisville, KY

Louisville pizza-makers themselves, quoted directly in a Louisville alt-weekly, say there is no distinct Louisville style despite periodic attempts to coin one.

Cincinnati, OH

Beyond a generic, loosely-defined "Ohio Valley" cold-topping association shared with Louisville and Steubenville, no distinct Cincinnati style was found.

New Castle, PA

No distinct codified style found in this research.

New Britain, CT

Bar pizza specifically was searched and not found to be a codified distinct style, despite New England's broader bar-pizza traditions elsewhere (see South Shore, § 05).

Erie is deliberately not listed here, despite also coming up empty for a codified "Erie style" — that finding gets its own full treatment in § 02, because it comes with a genuine positive finding (pepperoni balls) attached, and because it was the household's specific starting question rather than a background search.

§ 18

Sourcing Confidence Assessment

Across all 34 catalogued styles, sourcing quality varies meaningfully, and that variation is preserved here deliberately rather than smoothed into uniform confidence.

Confidence Tiers Across This Catalogue
Well-documented, high confidence

New York style, Neapolitan/VPN rules, Chicago deep dish (restaurant-of-origin solid, inventor genuinely disputed), Chicago tavern-style as a category, Chicago stuffed, Detroit style (including red-top specifically), New Haven apizza, St. Louis/Provel, Trenton tomato pie, Grandma pizza, Buffalo style, Quad City-style, Brier Hill, Ohio Valley/DiCarlo's, Columbus-style, Milwaukee tavern-style, Colorado mountain pie/Beau Jo's, California style, Rhode Island pizza strips, Massachusetts beach pizza, South Shore bar pizza, Greek/New England pizza, Utica tomato pie, and Hawaiian pizza's Canadian (not American) origin.

Moderately documented, some hedging

Sicilian-American vs. sfincione divergence (exact origin point hedged), Cleveland-style (trademark and Symon endorsement solid, deeper origin more general), Old Forge (living tradition well covered, 1926 date less anchored), Altoona-style (founding decade genuinely disputed across sources), Jersey Shore / boardwalk pizza (well corroborated across food/travel outlets and a dedicated regional-history book, but not treated as a formally codified style by any dedicated pizza-historian source the way New Haven or Old Forge are).

Thin or unconfirmed — flagged explicitly

"Erie style" as an independent codified tradition (the most important gap given this household's specific interest — see § 02), the Baltimore/Bob Beall origin story, New Mexico green chile pizza as a codified style, New Orleans/Gulf Coast pizza including the Catalina-dressing detail, Puerto Rican-style pizza generally, and the "blue-top" half of the Detroit red-top/blue-top question (appears to refer to pan color, not a real sauce-style counterpart).

Confirmed negative findings

Actively searched, no distinct style found: Seattle, Rochester NY, Kansas City, Louisville, Cincinnati (beyond generic Ohio Valley association), New Castle PA, and New Britain CT bar pizza specifically. See § 17.
