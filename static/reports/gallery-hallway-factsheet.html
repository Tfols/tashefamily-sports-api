2 · Single-loaded vs. double-loaded — the taxonomic distinction that matters most
Reading this sheet: the width comparison (top) shows why "gallery" and "hallway" feel like different words for different things — a code-minimum hallway and a true long gallery are roughly an order of magnitude apart in width, which is exactly why no single code-defined term covers both. The plan comparison (bottom) is the more useful architectural fact for renovation planning: a double-loaded corridor (rooms both sides) is structurally prone to a dark middle with no daylight source, while a single-loaded corridor (rooms one side, glazing/art wall opposite) is the layout that naturally supports gallery treatment — it's the historical long-gallery pattern at any scale, and also the layout that enables cross-ventilation, which double-loaded plans trade away for floor-area efficiency. Further Examples, Not Individually Detailed
Haddon Hall is popularly attributed to Robert Smythson (or son John) by the venue itself — Great British Life's regional-history coverage frames this as scholarly inference, not a documented commission.
Aston Hall, Birmingham (Jacobean, 1618–1635, architect John Thorpe) — Birmingham Museums Trust calls it "one of the finest long galleries in the country." A ≈136 ft length (incl. a 1700 addition) traces only to a synthesis of a history blog and a photography site, no URLs captured — genuine gap, unconfirmed lead.

Taxonomy
Gallery Corridor✓ This one
Gallery Corridor
Interior · art wall + glazed wall
An enclosed interior passage, wide enough to furnish and linger in, that carries a display wall opposite a window/view wall. The modern descendant of the long gallery.
This is what Tom is describing — see the photographic reference panel above
Loggia✕ Different thing
Loggia
Exterior · open on ≥1 side, columned
A covered, column-supported gallery open on at least one side, sharing the main building's roofline but accessed only from inside. Mediterranean/Renaissance origin — a transitional indoor/outdoor space, not an enclosed art corridor. [10]

Redlined out — wrong room type
Breezeway✕ Different thing
Breezeway
Exterior · open-sided connector
A roofed, open-sided passage connecting two separate structures (or two halves of a house), with no walls, windows, or conditioning. Simpler and more casual than a loggia — a connector, not a display space. [11][12]

Planning Considerations
Biggest Lever
Daylighting

Go single-loaded (rooms one side, glazing/view opposite) or layer in a skylight/clerestory as in the Buffalo renovation — double-loaded plans are structurally prone to a dark middle. [3][18]

Second-Order Benefit
Ventilation, Not Just Light

Single-loaded corridors also enable cross-ventilation and multi-sided daylight; double-loaded plans sacrifice this for floor efficiency and suit higher-density/higher-rise construction better.

Not Yet Confirmed
Footprint Cost

No verified industry rule of thumb for the square-footage tradeoff exists — going 36–48" to 54–72" roughly doubles the corridor's footprint for a given length, per the numbers above, but this wasn't independently costed.

Decide Early
Lighting Circuits

Plan track or dedicated picture-light circuits (fixtures 7–10 cm above frame, angled 30–35°) from schematic design — they need their own switching/dimming, not a retrofit. [22][25]

Lower-Authority Source, Flagged
Lighting Spec Detail

≈200–300 lux ambient with wall-washing accents; narrow beam (<20°) for small pieces, wider (30°+) for large canvases; concealed cove/valance preferred over visible fixtures. [25]

Framing-Stage Decision
Wall Blocking

For heavier/larger art, put continuous plywood blocking behind drywall at the hanging zone during framing — not a post-construction anchor fix. [23][24]

Genuine Gap
Flooring

No gallery-corridor-specific flooring guidance was found beyond general hallway durability advice — flagged as a real gap, not an oversight.

Baseline, Not Target
Code Minimum

36" (IRC) is the floor for any hallway. A true gallery corridor should be planned well above it from the earliest schematic — retrofitting width later means moving structural walls. [8]

Worth Bookmarking — Vault Candidates Flagged by the Research
Hardwick Hall & Montacute House (both National Trust, England) — the two best-preserved primary/original long-gallery examples, for historical grounding of the concept.
Gallery House (San Francisco) & the Wingfelder Gallery House (Buffalo) — the two strongest modern built precedents: houses actually organized around a gallery-as-spine, not just hallways decorated with art.
Girouard's Robert Smythson & the Elizabethan Country House (Yale University Press, 1983) — the academic monograph behind the best-supported Hardwick Hall figure; worth sourcing directly for primary-source-level confirmation.
