The 1993 Phillies finished 97–65 (.599), won the NL East for the first time since 1983, upset the heavily favored 104-win Atlanta Braves in the NLCS, and lost the World Series in six games to the defending-champion Toronto Blue Jays on Joe Carter's Game 6 walk-off home run off closer Mitch Williams. It was a "worst-to-first" turnaround from a last-place 1992 finish and remains one of the most fondly remembered teams in Phillies history despite the loss — the only winning season the club had in the entire 1990s.

§ 01

Key Players and Roster Highlights

The team was managed by Jim Fregosi.[1] Offensively, center fielder Lenny Dykstra had a career year, leading the National League in hits (194) and runs scored (143) while batting .305 with 19 home runs and 66 RBIs.[3] Catcher Darren Daulton hit 24 home runs and drove in 105 runs, his second consecutive 100-RBI season.[3] First baseman John Kruk batted .316 with 14 homers and 85 RBIs and became something of the team's chief personality — he famously described his teammates as "24 morons and one Mormon," a jab referencing the clean-cut Dale Murphy.[3][4]

Third baseman Dave Hollins drove in 93 runs for the second straight year, right fielder Jim Eisenreich hit .318 with only 36 strikeouts in 362 at-bats, and left fielder Pete Incaviglia hit 24 home runs with 89 RBIs in limited at-bats.[3]

The Pitching Staff

Curt Schilling went 16–7 with a 4.02 ERA and 186 strikeouts in the regular season, tying Tommy Greene (also 16 wins) for the team lead in wins.[5][3] Ben Rivera won 13 games, and Danny Jackson and Terry Mulholland each won 12.[3] The staff led the NL in complete games (24), innings pitched, and strikeouts.[3] Closer Mitch Williams ("Wild Thing") notched 43 saves with a 3.34 ERA during the regular season, though his wildness (44 walks in 62 innings) foreshadowed his postseason struggles.[3]

A memorable, if not strictly Phillies-season-specific, personality moment came at the 1993 All-Star Game at Camden Yards, where Kruk comically bailed out of the box against a 98-mph Randy Johnson fastball that sailed near his head — an at-bat that became a piece of enduring baseball lore about Kruk's persona.

§ 02

Regular Season: Turnaround and Identity

Coming off a last-place 1992 finish, the Phillies took the NL East lead on Opening Day 1993 and held it for all but a single day (April 9) the rest of the season, clinching the division on September 28 with a 10–7 win over Pittsburgh. The club led the league in at-bats, runs scored (877), hits, doubles, walks, on-base percentage, and total bases.[3]

Notable regular-season moments included an 8–0 comeback win over San Francisco (won 9–8 in 10 innings) and a marathon 20-inning game against the Dodgers that stretched past midnight into July 7.[3]

"Macho Row"

The team cultivated a distinctly unkempt, hard-nosed image — mullets, scraggly facial hair, and a "shaggy," "dirty" look — that earned the nickname "Macho Row" (a wink at the 1927 Yankees' "Murderers' Row").[3][4] This scrappy, blue-collar, beer-and-tobacco personality (a full book, William C. Kashatus's Macho Row: The 1993 Phillies and Baseball's Unwritten Code, was later written about the clubhouse culture) resonated strongly with Philadelphia fans and is widely credited with reinvigorating the fanbase and driving a spike in attendance the following year.[7][3] "Whoomp! (There It Is)" became the team's unofficial anthem during the run.[3]

Game 6: The Final At-Bat

Toronto closed out the series in Game 6 at SkyDome on October 23, 1993. The Phillies led 6–5 entering the bottom of the ninth. Williams walked Rickey Henderson to open the inning, retired Devon White on a fly ball, then allowed a single to Paul Molitor, putting the tying and go-ahead runs on base. With the count at 2–2 (accounts differ slightly on the exact final pitch — see § 06 for the discrepancy), Joe Carter hit a three-run walk-off home run, giving Toronto an 8–6 win and its second consecutive championship.[13][14]

It was the first World Series ever won by a trailing team on a walk-off homer and just the second Series-ending home run in history; broadcaster Tom Cheek's call — "Touch 'em all, Joe! You'll never hit a bigger home run in your life!" — is a landmark moment in Toronto sports history.[13]

"I made a mistake, and he hit the mistake. I let my team down today."

Mitch Williams, after Game 6 — who also acknowledged using an unfamiliar slide-step delivery on the at-bat[14]

Williams had also taken the loss in Game 4, and reporting indicates he received death threats after that game before the series even concluded — though this claim rests on weaker sourcing than the rest of this report (see § 06).[15][10] He was traded to Houston that offseason and never fully regained his earlier form.[14]

Blue Jays DH Paul Molitor was named World Series MVP, batting .500 (12-for-24) with two home runs, eight RBIs, and a Series record-tying 10 runs scored across the six games.[16]

§ 05

Final Outcome and Legacy

The Phillies finished 97–65, won the NL East and pennant, and lost the World Series 4–2 to Toronto.[1][2] Home attendance for the season was 3,137,674, fourth-highest in the majors.[1]

The 1993 season stands as the only winning season the Phillies had in the entire 1990s — the club did not post another winning record until 2001 — which has cemented 1993 as a singular, beloved anomaly in franchise history: a scrappy, colorful, worst-to-first team that captured the city's imagination even in defeat. The "Macho Row" identity and the Carter/Williams Game 6 ending remain touchstone reference points in both Phillies and Blue Jays franchise lore and in broader World Series history.[3][13]

Sourcing Confidence Assessment

Three items flagged explicitly — not smoothed over
Gap — direct-fetch blocked

Player season stats (Dykstra, Daulton, Kruk, Schilling, Williams, etc.) come from Wikipedia's season/player pages, which themselves derive from Baseball-Reference. Baseball-Reference's own site returned a 403/blocked response to automated fetch, so those figures are one layer removed from the primary database rather than confirmed against it directly.

Discrepancy — 2–1 vs. 2–2

Sources differ on whether the count on Carter's home run was 2–1 or 2–2. SABR's dedicated game recap and Mitch Williams's own account point to 2–2, treated in this report as the more reliable version. One Fox News summary states 2–1.

Weak sourcing — death threats claim

The claim that Williams received death threats after Game 4 comes from a single search-result summary of NPR reporting, not independently fetched or verified against the original transcript. Treat as a lead worth further verification rather than a fully confirmed fact.

The existence and significance of the "Macho Row" book (Kashatus) is confirmed via a bookseller listing; the book's contents were not read directly as part of this research.
